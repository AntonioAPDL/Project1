1      INTRODUCTION

River-flow forecasts support flood preparedness, drought monitoring, reservoir operations, and water-supply planning. Decisions
in each setting require an assessment of uncertainty as well as a forecast of flow. Hydrological uncertainty arises from imperfect
observations, model structure, parameters, and initial states; meteorological uncertainty enters through precipitation and other
2                                                                                                                      De Leon ET AL.

atmospheric forcing. These sources interact as uncertain weather inputs propagate through a hydrological system (Tarek, Arse-
nault, Brissette, & Martel 2021; Thiboult, Anctil, & Boucher 2016). A statistical forecasting method must therefore connect
the available hydrological and meteorological information to a predictive distribution that describes both routine conditions and
the tails relevant to floods and droughts.
   Operational forecasts use conceptual and physically based hydrological models, with calibration and deployment choices
shaping how meteorological forcing is translated into river-flow predictions (Demargne et al. 2014; Thiboult et al. 2016).
Several complementary sources of information are available for statistical correction of their output. Gauge observations measure
the target flow, retrospective products reconstruct past conditions, and forecast ensembles describe possible future conditions.
Operational ensemble services explicitly seek to represent uncertainty in hydrological predictions (Demargne et al. 2014); their
products nevertheless differ in spatial support, forecast horizon, update frequency, and model formulation. Retrospective records
allow systematic differences between an external product and the gauge record to be learned over time. Issued ensembles can
then inform future flow through the corresponding source-specific correction. The statistical task is to preserve these distinct
information roles while combining their contributions to the target predictive distribution.
   Forecast combination and statistical post-processing provide established approaches to calibration and combination (Siegert
& Stephenson 2019; Vannitsem et al. 2021; X. Wang, Hyndman, Li, & Kang 2022). Within hydrology, Bayesian model averag-
ing combines predictive distributions from multiple models (Duan, Ajami, Gao, & Sorooshian 2007). Ensemble model output
statistics (EMOS) links the parameters of a predictive distribution to ensemble forecasts (Gneiting, Raftery, Westveld, & Gold-
man 2005); hydrological applications include the correction of forecast location and spread (Hemri, Lisniak, & Klein 2015).
Quantile-based methods have also been used to merge precipitation products (Yumnam, Guntu, Rathinasamy, & Agarwal 2022),
while dynamic Bayesian predictive synthesis addresses combination over time (McAlinn & West 2019). Here the external prod-
ucts enter a shared quantile state-space model through source-specific dynamic discrepancies. A subsequent synthesis combines
predictions from independently fitted quantile models. The correction step therefore connects products to a common river-flow
process, and the synthesis step connects the separately fitted portions of its predictive distribution.
   We develop this correction-and-synthesis construction using dynamic quantile linear models (DQLMs) (Barata, Prado, &
Sansó 2022; Gonçalves, Migon, & Bastos 2020; Gourieroux & Jasiak 2008). Its methodological contribution is the integration
of three elements. A shared latent quantile process links gauge observations to retrospective and forecast products while estimat-
ing a dynamic discrepancy for each external source. A post-fit synthesis step combines the quantile-specific predictive output
into a single nondecreasing predictive quantile function. Finally, we adapt variational Bayes (VB) inference for the extended
DQLM (exDQLM), using Laplace–Delta approximations (C. Wang & Blei 2013) to update its non-conjugate scale and skew-
ness parameters. This computation makes the separate quantile fits practical for a long daily record. The application evaluates the
resulting configuration against its internal variants and external forecasting references; these comparisons delimit the empirical
gains from the construction.
   We illustrate the framework using daily San Lorenzo River flow at Big Trees, California. The analysis combines U.S. Geo-
logical Survey (USGS) observations with products from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF)
Global Flood Awareness System (GloFAS) and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) National
Weather Service (NWS). Five forecast origins support comparisons of predictive distributions over 28 days and over the com-
mon eight-day NWS horizon. Section 2 introduces the forecasting setting and data. Section 3 develops the model, computation,
and predictive synthesis. Section 4 gives the application specification and evaluation methods, Section 5 presents the results,
and Section 6 discusses their implications.

of lower quantiles are therefore relevant for drought conditions, whereas upper quantiles are relevant for flood risk.

3     BAYESIAN CORRECTION AND PREDICTIVE SYNTHESIS

For each target probability 𝑝0 , the model learns a latent river-flow quantile from observations and source-specific products. The
framework has two stages: dynamic correction combines the information channels within each quantile fit, and predictive syn-
thesis combines output across those fits. Bold symbols denote vectors or matrices; their dimensions are stated when introduced.
We write 𝐈𝑟 , 𝟎𝑟 , and 𝟏𝑟 for the 𝑟-dimensional identity matrix, zero vector, and one vector, respectively.


3.1     Quantile Likelihood

& Ghosh 2013; Yu & Moyeed 2001). We use its extended form, exAL𝑝0 (𝜇, 𝜎, 𝛾), developed by Yan, Zheng, and Kottas (2025).

3.4     Posterior Computation
The reported fits use mean-field VB. Conditional on the exAL augmentation variables, the observations are Gaussian and the
latent state follows a Gaussian evolution model. This structure permits a joint Gaussian approximation for the state trajectory,
computed by variational filtering and smoothing. The remaining factors describe the observation-specific augmentation variables
and each channel’s scale and skewness parameters.
   Coordinate-ascent iterations cycle through three groups of updates. Variational filtering and smoothing update the state means
and covariances using expected observation precisions and precision-weighted offsets from the likelihood factors. Generalized
inverse-Gaussian and positive-truncated normal updates refresh the augmentation factors. A VB Laplace–Delta (VB-LD) update
approximates the joint scale and skewness factor for each channel: the Laplace approximation matches the mode and curvature
on an unconstrained parameterization, and a second-order Delta approximation supplies the nonlinear expectations needed
by the other updates (C. Wang & Blei 2013). The iterations monitor changes in the evidence lower bound (ELBO), state,
scale, and skewness after required minimum update counts. Algorithms 3 and 4 give the update sequence and state recursions;
Appendix B.4 gives the approximation formulas.
   This adaptation extends the flexible DQLM approach of Barata et al. (2022), whose importance-sampling treatment of non-
conjugate parameters fixes the scale using an AL fit. The Laplace–Delta updates approximate scale and skewness jointly. They
provide the deterministic posterior approximation used throughout the comparisons below. Algorithms 1 and 2 give conditional
sampling equations for the historical observation model. All reported posterior summaries use VB; its accuracy relative to a
full-model Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) posterior has not been assessed.
   Separate target quantiles can be fitted in parallel using the estimation routines in the exdqlm package (De Leon, Barata,
Prado, & Sansó 2026). With the model-ready observations, retrospective products, forecast products, and covariates prepared,
representative publication runs for the seven-quantile set take about two hours end-to-end on a Linux server with 64 cores and
503 GiB of RAM. This is combined wall-clock time for fitting and forecasting; those stages were not timed separately. Runtime
depends on the hardware, implementation, series length, and state dimension, with repeated filtering and smoothing accounting
for the main computational cost.

4.1       Dynamic Components and Numerical Settings

to describe recurring within-year variation, and a slower rotation to permit multi-year variation. The slower component was
retained as an exploratory representation of multi-year variability. Its period is a model-specification choice, and we do not assign
it to a particular physical climate mechanism. Each source discrepancy has the same seven-state level-and-seasonal structure,
allowing source differences to vary at these time scales.

   We choose discount factors componentwise to reflect each component’s time scale and role. Recall that, in the discount-factor
formulation (Prado et al. 2021), a smaller value 𝑑 ∈ (0, 1] for a given state parameter implies larger state-evolution variance and
therefore greater adaptability of that component. The selected specifications use high, near-one discount factors for the trend,
seasonal, discrepancy, transfer, and covariate-effect states. These values preserve persistent long-run behavior while allowing
slow adaptation across the multi-decade observation window. Table A5 gives their exact values for the five reference fits; the
reproducibility bundle records all 45 model–origin specifications. The discrepancy factor multiplies the associated component
discount, so it is distinguished from the resulting effective discount.
   The transfer-persistence parameter 𝜆 was selected in a preliminary one-dimensional calibration step for the reference
extended-likelihood multivariate specification with an active transfer component. To avoid confounding transfer persistence
with state-evolution adaptability, the component discount factors were held fixed during this step. We considered the grid
𝜆 ∈ {0.60, 0.61, … , 0.99} and compared candidate values using CRPS for the median-quantile model over the pre-cutoff
observational window at the representative December 25, 2022 origin, which has the longest pre-origin archive among the
rolling-origin cases. The selected value was 𝜆 = 0.97, which was then held fixed across the reported quantile levels and forecast
origins. The resulting fixed persistence setting is used in the reported comparisons.
   For the forecast-window covariance prior, the parameter 𝜖 controls the prior weight assigned to the covariance structure
learned before the cutoff. Smaller values give a weaker anchor and allow greater adaptation during forecast propagation, while
larger values retain more of the pre-cutoff covariance structure. For the representative December 25, 2022 selected specification,
we set 𝜖 = 1 and 𝑐 = 1. These settings stabilize short-horizon forecast propagation while allowing the forecast-window state
evolution to adapt during a winter origin with substantial rainfall-driven uncertainty.


4.2     Forecast Origins and Product Alignment
The evaluation uses five forecast origins spanning relatively low-flow conditions and winter high-flow episodes: January 23,
November 12, and December 21, 2021, and May 11 and December 25, 2022. For each origin, a model-ready dataset aligns
the observations, retrospective products, issued ensembles, and covariate paths. These five cases cover contrasting hydrolog-
ical settings; they do not constitute a continuous daily hindcast. The separated origins also avoid repeatedly scoring strongly
overlapping forecast windows from one episode. Extending the evaluation requires recovering and aligning the corresponding
historical product versions, spatial extractions, members, and lead times.
   The fitting window includes USGS observations and retrospective products through 𝑇 . Forecast inference uses the external
ensembles for their available leads and the supplied transfer covariates. USGS values after 𝑇 supply verification. GloFAS issues
daily forecasts. For the hourly NWS product, the input construction retains the latest available issuance for each target time and
member at the origin, then aggregates the retained hourly values to daily resolution. The comparison uses eight daily leads for
the common NWS horizon and 28 leads for the GloFAS horizon, with each source entering only where its forecast members are
available.


4.3     Comparison Specifications
We compare nine Bayesian variants of the common state-space framework. These variants differ only in three respects: the
observation likelihood, whether the synthesis uses only the USGS observation channel or all source channels jointly, and whether
the transfer component remains active during the forecast window. We summarize them with the label 𝐿-𝑆-𝑇 , where 𝐿 ∈
{N, AL, exAL} denotes a Gaussian, asymmetric Laplace, or extended asymmetric Laplace observation likelihood, 𝑆 ∈ {U, M}
indicates whether the synthesis is univariate or multivariate, and 𝑇 ∈ {T0, T1} indicates whether the transfer component is
suppressed or retained during the forecast window.
   For the AL and exAL variants, we fit the framework separately at seven target quantile levels: 0.05, 0.20, 0.35, 0.50, 0.65,
0.80, and 0.95. The Gaussian variants correspond to normal dynamic linear models. Forecast skill is evaluated from the resulting
posterior predictive distributions by the mean CRPS. The comparisons use the VB procedure described in Subsection 3.4.
   For the predictive illustration and component summaries, we use exAL-M-T1: the extended likelihood, all source channels,
and the active transfer component. This is the reference specification selected from the 28-day comparison reported in Section 5.
Its single-origin display is illustrative; the tables summarize performance across the five origins.

4.4       External Forecasting References
We add three references with distinct practical roles. Seasonal climatology (CLIM-SEASONAL) is an empirical distribution of
historical USGS flow on the transformed scale. For each forecast date, it uses observations from May 29, 1987 through the origin
within ±15 calendar positions of the target month and day. Positions follow a circular calendar based on the leap year 2000,
retaining a separate February 29 position. This provides a season-specific distribution without using a hydrological forecast
product. Persistence (PERSISTENCE) is a point mass at the last observed transformed discharge, log(1 + 𝑥𝑇 ), repeated at every
lead. Its CRPS is the absolute forecast error.

5.5     Distribution-Score Sensitivity and Calibration
The exact empirical-distribution scores in Table 7 retain the aggregate transformed-scale ordering of the principal competitors.
For exAL-M-T1, mean CRPS is 0.20156 over 28 days and 0.29919 over eight days, corresponding to climatology-relative skill
of 0.68602 and 0.58965. The separate sample-size adjustment is small for the 2,000-draw quantile syntheses and larger for the
smaller Gaussian and raw ensembles (Appendix Table A6). This distinction avoids attributing a numerical-score convention to
a change in the fitted predictive distribution.
   Calibration provides a different assessment. The selected model’s central 95% interval covers 123 of 140 cases (87.86%) over
28 days and 29 of 40 (72.50%) over eight days (Table 8). The short-horizon misses are concentrated at the December 2021 and
December 2022 origins, where coverage is 1/8 and 4/8; the other three origins have 8/8 coverage. At 28 days those December
origins cover 21/28 and 19/28 cases (Appendix Table A8). AL-M-T1 has wider intervals and covers 132/140 and 35/40 cases,
with lower mean interval scores over both horizons. Narrow intervals and favorable average CRPS therefore do not establish
nominal predictive coverage.
   Figure 6 shows reliability at the seven target probabilities. For exAL-M-T1 over 28 days, the strict empirical frequencies at
probabilities 0.05, 0.50 and 0.95 are 6/140, 67/140 and 128/140. The upper value is below its nominal frequency, consistent
with upper-tail misses. Full counts, ties and origin-specific diagnostics accompany the article.
   The scoring scale also matters (Appendix Table A7). On the discharge scale, exAL-M-T1 has aggregate 28-day CRPS
6.26885 m3 /s and skill 0.39113. Over eight days, raw NWS has lower CRPS than exAL-M-T1, 6.63303 versus 7.49362 m3 /s,
reversing their aggregate transformed-scale ranking. Very large discharge-scale scores for N-M-T1 and exAL-M-T0 reveal sen-
sitivity to extreme predictive values. These findings limit a single scale-independent interpretation of the transformed-scale
advantage.

and the synthesis step.

6      DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

The proposed framework links gauge observations, retrospective products, and forecast ensembles through a dynamic quantile
process with source-specific discrepancies. This supplies a common statistical representation while preserving the sources’
22                                                                                                                    De Leon ET AL.

different roles and horizons. A separate synthesis of the fitted quantile models produces the marginal predictive distributions
used for forecast summaries and scoring. Laplace–Delta VB makes the extended-likelihood fits feasible for the multi-decade
daily record.
   In the San Lorenzo application, exAL-M-T1 has the lowest 28-day CRPS among the original framework variants and raw
products at all five reported origins. Within those comparisons, raw NWS is strongest at two winter origins over eight days,
while Bayesian variants are strongest at the other three. The added references show aggregate skill beyond seasonal climatology
for the selected quantile models and for EMOS-GLOFAS, while the EMOS comparator outperforms exAL-M-T1 in May 2022.
For a practitioner, these findings favor checking a single-product correction and simple references alongside the fuller source-
and-covariate construction, at the horizon relevant to the decision. An aggregate advantage across five cases does not imply the
best forecast at every origin or establish performance in another basin.
   The Gaussian transfer comparison and the component-removal results also show why predictive scores should be read along-
side fitting diagnostics. The full reference and nearly all reductions reach their iteration budgets without established joint
convergence, so the ablations support conditional sensitivity conclusions. They do not establish the independent necessity of
each component. Historical component summaries describe how the reference model allocates fitted variation; they are not
independent evidence of forecast improvement.
   The distribution checks further qualify the selected model’s advantage. Its favorable mean transformed-scale score coexists
with predictive undercoverage at the two December origins, and raw NWS has the better aggregate eight-day score on the
discharge scale. Calibration, sharpness, forecast horizon and scoring scale address different practical aspects of prediction. The
member-level likelihood additionally assumes conditional residual independence and a shared source scale across leads; the
raw-product error and spread diagnostics retained with the article describe its inputs without validating those assumptions.
Approximate VB parameter intervals remain distinct from predictive coverage, and their accuracy against full-model posterior
simulation is an open computational assessment.
   The framework depends on the external products, their alignment, and the supplied covariate paths. The five-origin evaluation
samples contrasting conditions; a denser evaluation would require a larger version-consistent historical forecast archive. The
present transfer inputs are deterministic summaries of uncertain hydrometeorological quantities. Future work could propagate
their uncertainty jointly, fit dependent quantiles directly, enrich the discrepancy dynamics, and extend the model to multiple
sites or basins.

         Compute Prior moments:
         𝐚𝑡 = 𝐃𝑡 𝐦𝑡−1
         𝐑𝑡 = 𝐃𝑡 𝐂𝑡−1 𝐃′𝑡 + 𝚲𝑡
         One-step-ahead forecast:
         𝐟𝑡 = 𝐇′𝑡 𝐚𝑡 + 𝐛𝑡
         𝐐𝑡 = 𝐇′𝑡 𝐑𝑡 𝐇𝑡 + 𝐎𝑡
         Compute Posterior moments:
         𝐦𝑡 = 𝐚𝑡 + 𝐑𝑡 𝐇𝑡 𝐐−1        𝑡
                                        (𝐘𝑡 − 𝐟𝑡 )
         𝐂𝑡 = 𝐑𝑡 − 𝐑𝑡 𝐇𝑡 𝐐−1         𝑡
                                        𝐇′𝑡 𝐑𝑡
     end for
     Step 2: Backward Smoothing
     for 𝑡 = 𝑇 down to 1 do
         if 𝑡 = 𝑇 then
              Set 𝑟(𝑘+1) (𝜼𝑇 ) ∶=  (𝐦𝑠𝑇 , 𝐂𝑠𝑇 )
              𝐦𝑠𝑇 = 𝐦𝑇
              𝐂𝑠𝑇 = 𝐂𝑇
         else
              Compute Smoothing moments:
              𝑟(𝑘+1) (𝜼𝑡 ) ∶=  (𝐦𝑠𝑡 , 𝐂𝑠𝑡 )
              𝐁𝑡 = 𝐂𝑡 𝐃′𝑡+1 𝐑−1      𝑡+1
              𝐦𝑠𝑡 = 𝐦𝑡 + 𝐁𝑡 (𝐦𝑠𝑡+1 − 𝐚𝑡+1 )
              𝐂𝑠𝑡 = 𝐂𝑡 + 𝐁𝑡 (𝐂𝑠𝑡+1 − 𝐑𝑡+1 )𝐁′𝑡
         end if
     end for
